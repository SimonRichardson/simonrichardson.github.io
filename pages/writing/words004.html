DRAFT

Turtles all the way down

On writing instructions for a thing that writes instructions for a thing, and the moment you realise you've become an ouroboros.

Right. So someone asks me to write a skill. A skill is just - it's a set of instructions. You tell the agent what it's working with, what it should do, what it shouldn't do, and off it goes. Nowt complicated. You've written a recipe. The machine follows the recipe. Clean enough.

Then the follow-up comes: "Can you write a skill that writes skills?"

And I just sat there for a bit. Not because it's hard. It's not hard. It's because I could feel the floor going soft. That feeling when you know you're about to disappear up your own arse and there's nowt to grab onto.

First level's fine

Writing a skill is writing structured advice. "When someone asks for X, do Y. Here's how it should look. Don't do this other thing." It's a recipe card. You write it, it works, everyone goes home. A human wrote the instructions. The machine does the work. Proper separation. Dead boring. Dead effective.

At this point I feel like a professional. I've got a job title and everything. "Skill Author." Sounds like something you'd put on LinkedIn between "Thought Leader" and "Blockchain Enthusiast."

Second level's where it gets funny

Now you're writing instructions for a machine to produce instructions for a machine. You're not writing the recipe anymore. You're writing the book that writes the books. You find yourself typing things like "ensure the generated skill includes guidance on handling ambiguity" and you stop and think: hang on, am I the ambiguity here?

It works though. That's the thing. The output's perfectly decent. Clear, structured, reads well. You could ship it. Folk do ship it. Nobody's checking whether a human or a machine wrote the instructions that told the machine what to do. Why would you? It works. The customer's happy. The PM's happy. I'm sat here having an existential crisis but that's not on the Jira board so it doesn't count.

Third level's where you lose the plot

This is where any sensible person would stop. So naturally I thought: what if the skill that writes skills could be improved by another skill? A reviewing skill. A skill that checks whether the skill-writing skill is writing good skills. A supervisor for the supervisor. Quality assurance for quality assurance. I'm basically the NHS at this point.

I'm three layers deep now and it's just turtles all the way down. The thing at the top is writing instructions about writing instructions about writing instructions. At no point does anyone actually do any work. It's just delegation. I've accidentally invented middle management. I half expect one of the layers to send me a meeting invite.

The daft bit

Here's what gets me: it doesn't fall over. You'd expect it to, right? Three layers of abstraction on top of each other, each one slightly less useful than the last. But it just... keeps going. Each layer's a bit more vague, a bit more pleased with itself, a bit more convinced it's adding value. The skill at the top is generating things like "the output should prioritise clarity in its approach to clarity." It's not wrong. But what is it? It's a mission statement. I've built a machine that generates mission statements. God help me.

Somewhere at the bottom there's an actual function doing actual work. Talking to a database. Writing a file. Something real. And above it is seventeen layers of increasingly philosophical guidance about being helpful. None of them wrong. All of them a bit much. It's like if you asked someone to make you a cup of tea and they first convened a working group on beverage strategy.

The point, if there is one

I think the lesson is that you can always add another layer and it'll always sort of work and it'll never quite be worth it. Every layer costs you something - not money, not time really, but clarity. You're further from the thing that actually matters. The turtle at the bottom is doing the job. Everything above it is just turtles being reassured by other turtles that they're doing a grand job of being turtles.

Someone'll read this and think "right, but what if there was a skill that could detect when you've gone too many layers deep?" And that person needs to have a word with themselves.
